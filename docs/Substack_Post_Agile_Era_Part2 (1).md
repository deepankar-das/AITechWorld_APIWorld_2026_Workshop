## A Ninety-Second Recap for Cold-Start Readers

Part 1 argued that the Agile contract has expired. Six failure modes are running in real engineering organizations right now: Ambition Freeze, Token Maxxing, Inadequate Specifications, Demo Theater, Review Collapse, and Agile Primitives Outrun. Gartner projects 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by 2027\. Faros AI's 2026 telemetry from 22,000 developers across 4,000 teams documented the Acceleration Whiplash: throughput up, quality down, at scale.

The Agentic Development Model, ADM for short, is the operating model I have been building and advising on for the last two years as the replacement contract. It rests on three Tier 1 primitives that arrange themselves in the causal order a single diff flows through: Cross-Model Convergence Review (the jury), RADAR (the risk-aware test selector), and the Convergence Loop (the eight-step process that drives residual defects to zero). Above the diff-level pipeline sits the delivery dashboard that ties per-diff operations to overall project progress.

Part 1 named these primitives with one-line definitions. This essay is the manual. If you want the diagnosis first, read Part 1\. If you are already convinced ADM is worth your attention, keep reading.

One scope note before the walkthrough. This essay covers the diff-level pipeline: how humans and agents coword on code that ships. There is a fourth, load-bearing question ADM addresses at the runtime layer, which is what a coworking agent is authorized to attempt in the first place and how the runtime refuses actions outside that authorization even when credentials would technically permit them. That is a security-architecture topic, not a coworking topic, and it deserves its own essay rather than a compressed section here. It will get one.

## The Three Primitives, in the Order a Diff Flows Through Them

Code generated by an agent enters the pipeline. Cross-Model Convergence Review reviews it. RADAR selects the risk-weighted subset of tests that has to run against it. The Convergence Loop drives what the jury flagged and what RADAR failed toward zero, iteration by iteration. The commit-attested gate admits the diff only after the Convergence Loop has terminated and the receipt has been bound to that specific change. The delivery dashboard aggregates the results at repo, product, and organization scope so leadership can see the roadmap the pipeline is executing against.

## The Cross-Model Convergence Review

The first primitive in the pipeline order is the one that answers Review Collapse without asking humans to read every diff. Human reviewers cannot keep up at agent velocity, and a single agent reviewing another agent from the same model family inherits the same blind spots as the code it is reviewing. The ADM's answer is a three-member LLM jury: three coding agents drawn from three different foundational-model families review each other's work in a round, so that Claude reviews what GPT produced, GPT reviews what Gemini produced, and Gemini reviews what Claude produced, or any equivalent rotation across three foundational-model families. Their findings are not majority-voted. They are converged into a single merged action list where every divergent finding carries an explicit owner, so that overlap is treated as redundancy and divergence is treated as signal. The human's role does not disappear. It moves to the convergence step, where a product owner or tech lead adjudicates the divergent findings the three jurors could not resolve on their own.

The research on why cross-family ensembling is the right shape here has firmed up quickly. A 2025 study of LLM ensembles for code showed that the theoretical ceiling of an ensemble sits roughly 83% above the best single model, that naive consensus voting captures almost none of that ceiling because it collapses into a popularity trap where the majority answer wins even when a minority juror is the only one that is right, and that a diversity-preserving aggregation strategy realizes about 95% of the theoretical ceiling on the same benchmarks ([Wisdom and Delusion of LLM Ensembles for Code, arXiv 2510.21513](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21513v1)). A separate line of work on LLM juries showed that a three-model panel drawn from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google beat a single larger judge across six evaluation datasets at roughly one-seventh the cost, and that same-family juries suffer from correlated errors, an "illusion of redundancy" where three jurors from the same model family miss the same defects together ([Verga et al., PoLL, as summarized by Orq.ai](https://orq.ai/blog/llm-juries-in-practice)). Cross-LLM verification specifically on secure-code generation has shown improvements of up to 47% over single-model baselines when the reviewer is from a different family than the author ([Multi-LLM Secure Code Evaluation, arXiv 2603.22717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.22717.pdf)).

The ADM leverages this by design. Each foundational-model family carries a distinct blind-spot profile, so a jury drawn one juror per family is the shape most likely to surface defects any single family would miss. I have run this Convergence Loop on my own code for months, and rather than assert a fixed taxonomy of which juror catches which class of defect, the clearest evidence for the underlying principle comes from a specific convergence incident on LexAxiom's own codebase. During what the team internally calls the K34 convergence cycle, Codex and Claude were both reviewing the same 445 failing Playwright tests. Codex anchored on the most dramatic signature in the raw logs, twenty-eight occurrences of a "stream is not readable" error, and attributed 95 percent of the failures to a body-parser and session race condition. Claude, reviewing the identical raw logs independently, produced a different, evidence-cited decomposition: pool exhaustion was the actual primary cause, responsible for 37,583 HTTP 503 responses, 84 percent of the failure volume, with the body-parser race a secondary factor accounting for a fraction of a percent. Fixing in Codex's order would have missed the dominant cause entirely. Fixing in the corrected order took the suite from 445 unexpected failures down to 10, a 97.8 percent reduction in a single cycle. No juror dominates, and the two are not interchangeable: a second instance of Codex reviewing its own output would likely have anchored on the same signature, because it shares the same blind spot with itself.

There is a second pattern worth naming, which is that the jurors themselves route work to each other. In the merged action list, a juror will regularly recommend that a specific finding be owned by one of the other two, on the stated grounds that the other model is stronger on that class of defect or holds more context on the affected surface. Claude will hand a numerical edge case to GPT. GPT will hand a cross-module refactor to Gemini. Gemini will hand a contract or API-shape question back to Claude. The jury, in other words, does not just produce findings. It produces an ownership graph across the three models, and that graph is often more accurate than any single model's self-assessment of what it is best at. The convergence step turns those divergent findings and cross-model handoffs into ownership, which is what makes the review actionable rather than advisory. And because the three jurors are already writing structured findings against the same specification and the same implementation plan, the merged action list drops directly into the failure ledger and the next Convergence Loop iteration, without a separate human triage step in between. That is what makes it a primitive of the operating model rather than a review technique bolted on top. A dedicated paper on the Cross-Model Convergence Review, laying out the defect taxonomy each juror surfaces, the cross-model ownership graph the jurors produce, and a structured study of how those patterns hold across model families and code domains, is in preparation and will be published as a follow-up to this essay.

## RADAR: Risk-Aware Dependency Analysis for Rapid Verification, with False-Negative-Rate Targets

The second primitive in the pipeline order is the test-selection paradigm the operating model uses to keep the Convergence Loop economically viable. Coverage as a metric is dead at agent velocity, for a reason that is easy to state and hard to escape. Full-suite regressions on every agent-authored diff are neither fast enough to feed the execution engine nor cheap enough to run inside any sane cloud budget. Skipping tests is not an option either, because skipping is what produces the bad-quality exposure that surfaces in production. The team either runs everything and stalls, or runs a subset and hopes, and both failure modes end at the same place. What is needed is a selector that runs exactly the right subset for a given diff, with a defensible argument for why the subset is sufficient, and with declared floors that no selector can undercut. That selector is what the operating model calls RADAR.

RADAR scans the blast radius of an agent's change across multiple dimensions, not just the direct code dependency graph. It considers the file the change touched, the module and package boundaries it crossed, the data structures it read or wrote, the API contracts it exposed or consumed, the runtime paths it altered, the observed historical failure correlations of the code around it, and the invariants declared at the system level as always-on. The output is not a single dependency-graph slice. It is a risk-weighted list of tests, each carrying the reasons it was selected and the dimension of the blast radius it covers. A test selected because it exercises a data-structure invariant carries different weight than a test selected because it happens to import a file the diff touched, and RADAR keeps those reasons legible in the run folder so a reviewer can see not just which tests ran but why each one was chosen.

The primitive that gives RADAR its teeth is the false-negative-rate target, declared per test priority. A false negative in a test is a run that passes when the underlying code is actually broken. Every test has a false-negative rate that can be measured empirically over its history: how often, when the code it covers was later found to be defective, did this test pass anyway. Traditional coverage-driven testing does not track this at all, because coverage is a property of what the test touches, not of what the test catches. RADAR binds each test to a false-negative-rate target that depends on the test's priority tier. Tests attached to critical invariants carry a target near zero, meaning the test's historical false-negative rate has to be at or below a small declared threshold or the invariant is deemed insufficiently protected. Tests attached to high-blast-radius code carry a higher tolerated target, and tests attached to low-priority code carry the loosest. The declared target is what turns "we have a test for that" into a measurable, auditable claim about how well the test actually protects the code it is supposed to protect. If a test's false-negative rate drifts above its target, RADAR flags the test for repair, augmentation, or replacement, not the code it covers.

Alongside the false-negative-rate targets, RADAR enforces mandatory floors for critical invariants. A critical invariant is a system-level property the operating model has declared must never be violated: no data loss on the primary write path, no authorization bypass on any privileged operation, no unbounded resource consumption in any request handler, no silent corruption of persisted state, and so on for whatever set of invariants the team has committed to protect. Each critical invariant has one or more anchor tests, and RADAR is forbidden from selecting away those anchor tests on any diff, regardless of what the blast-radius analysis says. The floor is what prevents an intelligent selector from getting too clever and skipping a test that would have caught a catastrophic failure. Intelligence in test selection is welcome. Intelligence that lets a critical-invariant regression through is not, and the floor is the mechanism that names the line.

RADAR feeds the run folder as much detail as it produces. Every run folder holds, alongside the receipt and the executor logs, the selection RADAR made for that diff, the reasons per test, the false-negative-rate target and observed rate per selected test, and the list of critical-invariant anchor tests that were enforced. The composition with the rest of the operating model is direct. The Convergence Loop runs RADAR-selected tests each iteration, because a full-suite run per iteration is not economically viable and a random subset per iteration is not epistemically defensible. The commit-attested gate accepts a receipt only if the receipt was produced against a RADAR selection whose anchor tests were all enforced and whose false-negative-rate targets were all met. The cross-model jury reads the run folder including RADAR's selection reasons, so a juror flagging a suspect area can cross-check whether the tests RADAR ran on that area actually cover the class of defect the juror is worried about. Together, they turn testing from a stage in a pipeline into a continuous property of the diff, executed inside the Convergence Loop rather than after it.

## The Convergence Loop

The Convergence Loop is the mechanism that takes what the cross-model jury found and what RADAR ran against the diff and drives both to zero, iteration by iteration, before the diff ever reaches human review. It sits at the center of the operating model, and it deserves its own naming because it is the primitive that finally makes left-shift real rather than aspirational. Left-shift has been the stated goal of every quality movement for two decades, and it has never worked at scale, because there was no mechanism strong enough to force the residual defect count to zero before human review. Shift-left in the pre-AI world meant running tests earlier or reviewing specs sooner, but the pipeline always terminated on a schedule, not on a quality gate. Sprints ended. Deadlines hit. Code shipped with known defects because there was no exit condition tied to quality. The Convergence Loop inverts that. It terminates when residual defects reach zero, not when the calendar says so. That is a categorical change, not an incremental one.

The reason the Convergence Loop is possible now is that agent velocity changes the economics underneath it. In the human-paced world, iterating until residual defects reach zero was theoretically correct and economically impossible, because each iteration cost days or weeks of human time and the budget collapsed before residual defects reached zero. Teams had to ship with residual defects because the alternative was to never ship. Agent velocity moves the cost per iteration from human-days to agent-minutes and tokens. The Convergence Loop that could not be run in the human-paced world has to be run in the agent-paced world, because leaving residual defects in a diff before it reaches a human reviewer wastes the one scarce resource the operating model still has, which is human reviewer attention. This is the sense in which the Convergence Loop is a genuine invention of the agentic era rather than a rename of an older idea. It is the loop the previous economics could not afford.

The Convergence Loop is also what makes the cross-model jury and the commit-attested gate load-bearing rather than decorative. The jury outside the Convergence Loop produces a review artifact that sits in a file. The jury inside the Convergence Loop produces the input to the next iteration, which produces the next merged action list, which produces the next iteration, until the list is empty. The jury outside is a review; the jury inside is a defect-elimination engine. Symmetrically, the commit-attested gate without the Convergence Loop upstream of it is a filter that fires late, catching diffs that should never have been submitted in the first place. The gate downstream of a Convergence Loop that has already driven residual defects to zero is verifying a diff that deserves to pass, which is the shape a gate has to have in order to move trust rather than to slow the pipeline. Neither the jury nor the gate is fully useful without the Convergence Loop. The Convergence Loop is the primitive the other two orbit.

### The Eight Steps of the Convergence Loop

It is worth walking through the Convergence Loop as a procedure, because the composition of RADAR, the jury, and the fix-and-verify discipline is what gives the Convergence Loop its shape. The Convergence Loop runs the following eight steps in order, and both product code and test code are first-class citizens throughout.

**Step one.** The coding agent hands off the code changes and the full context, meaning the user prompts, the context, the codebase, and the diffs for both the product code and the test suite. This is what enters the Convergence Loop, carrying both the product and test surfaces.

**Step two.** The three-member cross-family LLM jury peer-reviews the diff in two parallel lanes, one for product code and one for test code, against the prompts, context, and codebase supplied in step one. On product code, the jurors converge on correctness, missing cases, structural issues, and alignment with the prompts and context. On test code, the jurors converge on coverage adequacy against the new surfaces, correctness of assertions, presence of critical-invariant anchor tests, and absence of test-shape defects such as over-mocking, happy-path-only coverage, or flake-prone timing. The merged review carries findings on both lanes.

**Step three.** The test-code changes the jury approved are built and added to the RADAR-managed test suite, so that the suite the Convergence Loop is about to execute reflects both the existing regression tests and the newly-added tests for the new surfaces. RADAR then runs against that updated suite, producing a risk-weighted list of tests to execute and capturing failing tests and residual coverage gaps as the current issue set.

**Step four.** Jury findings and RADAR failures combine into a work-item list carrying three lanes: product-code fixes, test-code fixes, and coverage-gap additions. Each item carries an explicit owner, whether that owner is one of the three jurors, a specialist agent, or a human.

**Step five.** Each owner resolves its assigned items in parallel across the three lanes. The no-rerun logs and traces produced during the failing RADAR run and the jury review carry enough detail for each fix to be made in one shot, without a reproduce-and-rerun-with-more-logging round trip.

**Step six.** Each owning agent executes the specific tests that cover the work items it just fixed, and confirms that those tests now pass.

**Step seven.** The three-member cross-family LLM jury reviews the fixes produced in step five across all three lanes, against the original prompts, context, and diff, and the fix owners incorporate the jury's review comments before the Convergence Loop advances.

**Step eight.** Once every owner has closed its work items and incorporated jury review comments, a follow-up RADAR run is triggered against the updated diff, either targeted to the surfaces the fixes touched or full if the fixes were structural enough to warrant it. The jury re-examines any surface where fixes landed, checking whether the fix phase introduced new product behavior that needs new tests. If the follow-up run captures new issues or the jury finds new gaps, those issues re-enter the Convergence Loop at step two with the updated diff, work items are re-assigned, and the sequence repeats. If the follow-up RADAR run comes back clean and the jury signs off on coverage adequacy against the current diff, the Convergence Loop terminates and the diff is handed off to the commit-attested gate.

Tests are code, and a jury that reviews product code without also reviewing the tests that guard it is a jury doing half its job. This is what makes the false-negative-rate targets from the RADAR section enforceable rather than aspirational: the coverage those targets are measured against is itself the product of cross-model convergence, not a byproduct of the same agent that wrote the code under test.

This is the inner discipline of the Convergence Loop, and it is what prevents the failure mode where an agent claims to have fixed an issue but has actually introduced a regression somewhere adjacent, or has quietly weakened the test that was guarding that surface. Each fix carries its own RADAR pass, its own jury review, its own confirmation that the false-negative-rate targets and critical-invariant floors still hold, and its own trace in the run folder. The Convergence Loop closes one issue at a time, but it closes each one with the same rigor the whole loop applies to the diff, and the Convergence Loop as a whole terminates only when a follow-up RADAR run comes back clean against the diff as it now stands and the jury has signed off on coverage adequacy.

### The Convergence Curve as the Metric That Replaces Coverage

The Convergence Loop also reframes the metric that matters. Coverage is dead at agent velocity, which is the observation RADAR is built on. What replaces coverage as the primary quality signal is not another single scalar. It is the shape of the convergence curve for a given diff: how many iterations were needed to drive residual defects to zero, and what defect population was resolved at each iteration. A team whose Convergence Loop terminates in two iterations on average is operating differently from a team whose Convergence Loop takes fifteen, and both are operating differently from a team whose Convergence Loop never terminates and eventually times out on a diff that has to be abandoned. That is a real, comparable operational signal, and no framework before this one has named it as the signal that matters at agent velocity.

In one real engineering org I have worked with, the Convergence Loop took an outcome with 2,094 initial failures down to 445 after the first iteration, 10 after the second, and 0 after the third. The residual defect ratios, 0.21, then 0.022, then 0.0, are not magic. They are the output of treating convergence as a phase with its own gates, its own ownership, and its own metrics.

## The Commit-Attested Gate

The audit log is the input worth pausing on, because it is the one the current operating model handles the most loosely and where a small change in mechanism produces the largest movement in trust. Most teams today treat the CI run and the commit as two loosely coupled events. Tests pass on one build. A different diff lands on another. Nothing in the repository binds the passing run to the specific change that shipped, which is exactly the class of failure that lets a plausible-looking commit merge on the strength of an unrelated green build. In a human-paced world this was a hygiene problem that a diligent reviewer could catch. At agent velocity, with an order of magnitude more commits per day, a diligent reviewer cannot catch it, and the class of failure becomes a load-bearing weakness of the operating model rather than a minor oversight.

The mechanism that closes this is a per-commit gate that admits a commit only if it carries, in its trailer, an attestation identifying a specific test run whose recorded diff matches the staged diff and whose verdict is a pass. The primitive is called a commit-attested gate, and the attestation itself is called a receipt. A local hook rejects commits that arrive without one, and an independent server-side hook re-verifies the receipt against the pushed diff before accepting it into the repository. The receipt is not stored in a vendor system. It is written into a run folder next to the code, hashed against the diff it attests, and governed by a retention policy the team owns. The run folder holds the test selection RADAR produced for that diff, the parallel execution plan, the executor logs and traces, the resulting verdict, and the receipt itself. The retention policy is the team's contract with its own auditors: how long the run folders live, when they are compacted, what evidence is preserved after compaction, and where the compacted evidence is anchored so that a receipt referenced years later can still be verified against the diff it attested.

Nothing about the mechanism is exotic. What is new is that the primitive is named, specified once, and required by the operating model, so that every commit in the repository has the same shape of proof behind it. The receipt schema is the same across every team that adopts the model. The run folder layout is the same. The retention policy has a canonical shape teams parameterize rather than reinvent. That uniformity is what turns an ad-hoc CI-and-commit coupling into an auditable trail an organization can stand behind. It is also what makes the gate the right exit for the pipeline the previous three sections described. The jury reviewed the diff. RADAR selected and ran the risk-weighted tests against it. The Convergence Loop drove residual defects to zero. The gate admits the diff, and only that diff, on the strength of a receipt bound to the exact content the Convergence Loop terminated on. The gate is the exit; the composition upstream of it is what makes the exit meaningful.

## The Delivery Dashboard

The three Tier 1 primitives cover the pipeline a single diff flows through. Above the diff-level pipeline sits an aggregation view that reports on the roadmap the pipeline is executing against, and the operating model calls that view the delivery dashboard. The dashboard is not itself a Tier 1 primitive. It is the artifact that ties per-diff operations to overall project progress and gives leadership the CEO-openable view the vendor tools currently hallucinate.

Each row in the delivery dashboard is a large feature or piece of work drawn from the implementation-plan file. Each row carries an owner, a percent-completion figure, the current convergence status of the diffs assigned to that row, the count of receipts that have already been admitted through the gate against that row, and the value-per-token ratio computed from the token log and the outcome recorded in the plan. The dashboard is sourced directly from the repo-resident markdown that the agents are executing against, not from a vendor database populated by an integration that manufactures ticket state after the fact. That means the CEO's morning open shows the same source of truth the agents are working from, updated in place as they execute, without an intermediate reporting layer that lags or drifts. It also means the dashboard is diff-able, reviewable, and version-controlled the same way the code is, because it is generated from files that live next to the code.

The dashboard is scoped to whatever aggregation the reader needs. A single-repo dashboard shows the delivery status of that repo's implementation plan. A product-level dashboard aggregates delivery status across the multiple repos that make up a product. An organization-level dashboard aggregates across the products the organization ships. The generation logic is the same at every level of aggregation, because every level reads the same repo-resident markdown files and rolls them up. The dashboard is the answer to the question Justin Bellero and Mickyarun's write-ups implicitly raise but do not resolve, which is what the leadership view of a repo-resident operating model looks like when the vendor dashboard is gone and the CEO still needs a screen they can open in a board meeting. The delivery dashboard is that screen.

## An Operating Model, Not a List of Primitives

It is worth stepping back from the individual primitives to name the shape they form together, because ADM is not a list of good ideas. It is an end-to-end operating model with a single load-bearing mechanism at its center and the other primitives arranged around it. The Convergence Loop is the center. The cross-model jury feeds it the findings that populate each iteration's action list. RADAR selects the tests it runs, so it is verifying against the risk-weighted subset that matters rather than a full-suite regression that no team can afford at agent velocity. The commit-attested gate closes the pipeline by admitting only diffs whose receipts prove the Convergence Loop terminated cleanly on that specific change. The failure ledger absorbs every defect the Convergence Loop ever resolved and turns it into a rule the system enforces on the next run, so termination gets shorter over time rather than longer. The two-state closure resolves the whole outcome as delivered or explicitly abandoned once the pipeline has closed and the gate has admitted the diff. Value-per-token is the ratio the operating model reports on, once the outcome has been closed and the token log has been sealed into the run folder.

That composition is what the model asks an organization to adopt.

**Primitives.** Outcomes, specifications, parallel lanes, failure-ledger entries, commit-attested gates, two-state closures, and value-per-token ratios.

**Artifacts.** Implementation-plan files, test template instances, run folders, receipts, ownership tables, agent ownership records, instruction-file hierarchies, retention policies, value-per-token reports, specification files, and the delivery dashboard that rolls those artifacts up into a leadership-legible view at repo, product, or organization scope.

**Procedures.** The outcome review that opens the pipeline, the morning briefing that reads what the agents did overnight, the Convergence Loop that runs until residual defects reach zero, the cross-model jury that produces the merged action list feeding each iteration, the RADAR-selected test run whose receipt attests each commit, the milestone verification record that closes the pipeline, the incident-to-rule pipeline that hardens the system after every failure, and the continuous specification grooming that keeps the pipeline deeper than execution can drain it.

**Outcomes.** Diffs that reach human review with zero residual defects, commits that carry proof against the exact diff they attest, ceremonies sized to a tempo the team actually runs at, a leadership view sourced from the same artifacts the agents are executing against, and a vocabulary the vendor tools cannot yet describe.

**ROI.** A productivity multiplier of three to five times in year one and ten to twenty times in year two, a defensible answer to the trust question every executive is currently asking, an audit trail worth standing behind, and a cancellation rate that lives on the far side of the 40% Gartner reported for teams still running on Agile primitives.

## What Happens to the Ceremonies

Before describing what running ADM week to week looks like, it is worth walking through what happens to the Agile ceremonies themselves when execution runs at five to ten times human speed. Every ceremony in the Agile playbook was calibrated to a specific human tempo. When the tempo changes by an order of magnitude, each ceremony breaks in a different way, and each one breaks for a different structural reason. Naming those reasons is useful, because the replacement contract has to answer each one.

**Sprint planning breaks first.** Two weeks used to be a reasonable planning horizon because two weeks was a reasonable execution horizon. It is neither anymore. Agents finish two weeks of human work in an afternoon and then sit idle, waiting for the next thing to be well-defined enough to work on. The bottleneck moves upstream from execution to specification. Planning stops being a periodic ceremony and becomes a continuous specification pipeline running weeks or months ahead of execution. If your specification pipeline is two sprints deep, your execution engine will drain it in a week and stall.

**The daily standup dissolves.** Fifteen minutes each morning to sync on human progress made sense when humans were the workers and their progress moved a story point at a time. Agents do not need a standup. They do not have blockers a colleague can unblock in the hallway. What used to be a standup becomes something else entirely: a morning briefing that reviews what the agents completed overnight, what needs a human decision or approval today, and what the specification pipeline should push next. It is closer to an air traffic controller's shift-change briefing than to the round-the-room ritual most teams still run at 9:15 every morning.

**Testing stops being a phase, and the testing strategy has to change with it.** Testing was historically sized against human-generated code, which arrived in human-sized chunks at human speed. Agent-generated code arrives at five to ten times the volume and at a faster pace. Scrum theory always required tests to pass inside the sprint, and that was reasonable when a sprint carried a handful of human-authored changes. It is no longer reasonable. Testing cannot be executed the way it has been done traditionally inside a sprint. There are too many changes moving through the sprint at once, and any attempt to test them all in the classical way will not merely spill work into the next sprint; the classical method will never catch up with the volume and velocity of AI-authored pull requests. Each sprint close takes longer than the one before it, because the untested backlog keeps accreting, and the time-box that defined the sprint in the first place stops holding. What was a two-week iteration becomes a three-week iteration, then a four-week iteration, then not an iteration at all.

Running the full regression suite for every agent-authored change is not an option either. It is neither economical nor fast enough to keep the execution engine fed. It is also genuinely expensive at scale, since a full-suite run on every pull request hogs compute, storage, network, and other cloud infrastructure resources on a duty cycle no team ever budgeted for. The team either runs everything and stalls the pipeline while the cloud bill climbs, or runs a subset and hopes. Neither is acceptable, because skipping tests creates the other problem the team cannot afford, which is a bad-quality exposure that will surface in production.

The defensible answer has three parts, and none of them work alone. The first part is smart, automated selection, which the operating model handles through RADAR. RADAR is developed in its own section above, so the short form here is that it selects tests on multi-dimensional blast radius, binds each test to a false-negative-rate target that depends on the test's priority tier, and enforces mandatory floors for critical invariants so that no selector can silently skip a test that would have caught a catastrophic failure.

The second part is an execution framework that runs the selected tests in a dynamic, just-in-time, maximally parallel configuration. It has to be dynamic because the corpus of tests selected will be different for every pull request, since every pull request touches a different subset of the code and its dependencies. A fixed parallel plan cannot be prepared in advance; the plan has to be computed per pull request from the selection RADAR produced, and the shape of the parallel fan-out has to size itself to that plan, not to a fixed pool sized for peak load. Just-in-time because the executors are provisioned when the pull request arrives and released when it lands, not held idle between runs. Maximally parallel because the pipeline is designed to saturate whatever infrastructure the team has budgeted for testing, in the window the pull request has to clear.

The third part is no-rerun logs. The logs and traces produced by the execution framework have to be designed and instrumented so that every failure is fully diagnosable from the first run. If a test fails, the log, the trace, the input, the environment state, the state at the point of failure, and the causal chain leading up to it are all captured in that single execution. No engineer, human or agent, should ever have to say "reproduce it and rerun with more logging" to fix an AI-authored regression. That was tolerable when a sprint carried ten changes and each rerun cost a coffee break. It is fatal when a sprint carries a thousand changes and each rerun costs another dynamic fan-out. No-rerun logs are what let a failing test be fixed in one pass instead of three, and closing in one pass is what lets the Convergence Loop actually converge inside the time and cost envelope the pipeline has.

Intelligent selection without dynamic parallel execution is still too slow. A static parallel pool without intelligent selection still wastes the infrastructure. A parallel run without no-rerun logs pays for the compute and still cannot diagnose what failed. Only all three together give full coverage of what changed inside the time and cost envelope the pipeline can actually afford. The output of this testing discipline is what the Convergence Loop then consumes as one of its inputs, alongside the jury review and the coding agent's diff. Testing is no longer a stage. It is the fabric.

**Code review becomes the binding constraint.** This is the hardest structural break in the whole operating model. Human review at agent velocity is what Faros documented breaking in the field: median review time up 441.5%, 31.3% of code reaching production with no review at all. Review has to be redesigned around what humans can actually do at this volume. The diff sent to a human has to be small, focused, and worth their attention. Everything upstream, including the Convergence Loop, exists to earn the right to send a reviewable diff to a human. When review is the constraint, every ceremony upstream of review either serves that constraint or it goes.

**Dependency resolution moves out of the standup.** In the old model, dependencies were resolved through conversation between humans across teams. Alice waits for Bob's API. Bob waits for Carol's schema. Everyone Slacks each other, and the standup is where the blockers surface. Agents cannot Slack. Dependency resolution has to become explicit, declared in the specification, and resolvable programmatically. Contracts between services, between teams, and between agents themselves all become first-class artifacts, not ad hoc negotiations. The specification layer carries the dependency graph. The standup is no longer where dependencies surface, because by the time a human would notice, the agent has already been idle for hours waiting on something the spec should have declared upfront.

**Backlog grooming becomes a full-time discipline.** Grooming used to be an hour a week to keep the next couple of sprints tidy. Now the backlog is the specification pipeline, and it has to be groomed continuously, because execution eats it faster than a weekly ceremony can refill it. Grooming becomes a full-time job for specific roles, and it is one of the most important roles in the new operating model. The person who keeps the specification pipeline deep, well-defined, and ahead of execution is doing the load-bearing work of the whole system.

**Retrospectives change their questions.** Retros are the one ceremony that still makes sense, but the questions they ask have to change. It is no longer "how did the team do this sprint." It is: did the specification pipeline stay ahead of execution, did the Convergence Loop actually converge, did guardrails hold, did the token budget align with customer value delivered, and where did trust between humans and agents get broken this cycle. The new retrospective is a review of the operating model's health, not the team's velocity.

Every one of these changes is a consequence of a single fact: execution now runs faster than the specification, review, coordination, and reflection layers were built to keep up with. The replacement contract has to rebuild each of those layers at the new tempo.

## What Running ADM Looks Like

Imagine, concretely, a single engineering organization a year into running on ADM. What does the week look like?

The week starts with a planning cadence that is no longer a two-week sprint planning session. It is an outcome review. The product owner brings a customer outcome, a thing a named customer will sign off on at the end of it, and the team's role is to specify it. The specification is the artifact that takes the most human time in the week. The team writes the customer outcome, the acceptance criteria, the blast-radius assessment, the classification on a small set of axes (autonomy, reversibility, blast radius, specification fidelity, review cost), and the cost-tier routing decision (which class of model gets the work). The specification is reviewed by a human conductor before any agent runs.

When the spec is approved, the work is dispatched. Implementation happens in hours, not weeks. The work passes through the Convergence Loop: every observed failure produces a new test, the jury reviews product and test code in parallel lanes, RADAR runs the risk-weighted subset, owners fix the work items in parallel, the jury reviews the fixes, and the residual defect count drops by an order of magnitude per iteration until it reaches zero. Only then is a diff produced and capped at a size humans can review. A human approves the diff. The commit-attested gate admits it on the strength of a receipt bound to the exact content the Convergence Loop terminated on.

At the customer outcome level, the pipeline closes when the customer signs off. The ratio that gets tracked at the board level is customer value delivered over token cost spent. Overruns on the token budget reopen the spec, because token overruns are diagnostic, not disciplinary. They signal that the spec was wrong or scoped incorrectly.

The roles in this organization have changed. The senior crafts of 2026 are spec writing, taxonomy design, guardrail engineering, routing strategy, convergence engineering, and review systems. Implementation was never the craft. It was the proof that the engineer had the craft. The proof has changed. The craft has not.

Notice what is missing from this week. There is no sprint board. There is no velocity chart. There is no story-points estimation ritual. The tools most engineering organizations open every morning, Jira, VersionOne, Linear, Azure DevOps, Asana, Monday, Rally, were designed for a world in which the worker was a human and the unit of planning was a human's day. Those tools are excellent at what they were built for. They were simply not built for this. The teams running the post-Agile week are not using these tools the way they were designed to be used. They are working around them, or they are routing the actual agent work through something else and using the legacy tool as a reporting layer for the executives who still ask to see the burndown chart. The tools that encode the Agile contract will need to be rebuilt, or replaced, before the post-Agile operating model can run the way it should. That work has not happened yet. It is one of the largest greenfield opportunities in enterprise software in the next three years.

The hard part of running this operating model is not the agents. The agents are willing. They will work all night. The hard part is the organizational change required to use them: promoting spec writers, retraining engineers as conductors, re-budgeting by outcome instead of by engineer, and making the conductor role real.

The reason 40% of these projects will be cancelled is not because the agents do not work. It is because the org could not change fast enough to use them.

## What I Would Tell Three Audiences This Week

Three audiences are likely reading this essay, and each one has a different ask.

**To practitioners, engineers, architects, the people who actually drive the agents.** Pick one outcome this week. One. Then run it through the pipeline end to end:

1. Write a real spec before any code is generated.  
2. Classify the outcome on a small set of axes that matter to your work, at a minimum reversibility and blast radius.  
3. Route the work to a model class deliberately.  
4. Run the work through the Convergence Loop until the residual defects hit zero, then send the diff to human review.  
5. Run the whole thing through every guardrail you can articulate, even if you fake the gates with checklists.  
6. Measure the result: customer value delivered against token cost spent.

If you do this for four weeks, you will know whether the operating model works for your team better than any essay can tell you.

**To engineering leaders, directors, VPs, the people who staff and govern engineering organizations.** Audit your last ninety days. Every incident, every cancelled project, every customer escalation. Map each one to a missing guardrail or a skipped Convergence Loop. The pattern is almost always visible by incident number five. Then do three things, in this order:

1. Stand up observability first, because you cannot manage what you cannot see.  
2. Re-budget by outcome instead of by engineer.  
3. Promote your best spec writers while quietly demoting the fast typists.

The culture follows comp. Until you change comp, the culture will not change. And if you are not measuring incidents per merged PR, code churn, and median review time month over month against AI adoption, the three metrics where the Acceleration Whiplash shows up first, you are going to find out about Review Collapse from your customers, not from your dashboards.

**To executives, CEOs, boards, the people who issued the AI mandate.** You already did the first part. You issued the mandate. The harder part is the part most of you have not done. Point the arrow. Name the 10x larger problem your now-multiplied workforce should be solving. If your engineering organization just got 10x more productive and the only thing you can think to do with the multiplier is run it against the same backlog you had last year, you have not done your job. You have shifted the cost of your strategic ambiguity onto your CFO, who will eventually convert it to layoffs. The direction is your job. Nobody else in the organization can set it.

Then issue the directives that have to accompany the mandate:

1. Fund the specification layer of your organization, not the model layer. The models are commodities now, and the specs are not.  
2. Set the token budget at the outcome level, not the engineer level, and treat overruns as planning signals rather than cost signals.  
3. Ask your CFO to track a single new ratio: qualified token spend over total token spend, where qualified means tied to an approved outcome that converged. That ratio catches Token Maxxing before it shows up on the income statement.  
4. Ask your engineering org, by name, which guardrails they have in place. If they cannot list them, you are funding the 40% column.

Without those, the mandate is just pressure, and the ambition, direction, specification, alignment, judgment, review, and human-and-agent coworking model that have to accompany it will never get built.

## The Bottom Line for Part 2

The three Tier 1 primitives of ADM answer three different classes of failure. Cross-Model Convergence Review catches errors in what the agent produced. RADAR catches errors in whether the code works. The Convergence Loop catches errors in whether the tests are adequate. Together they form the operating model coworking teams need for shipping code, and the delivery dashboard above them makes the whole thing legible to the org above the engineering line.

None of the three is a silver bullet. Each removes one class of rework. What earns the productivity multiplier is the composition. Each iteration of the Convergence Loop starts from a diff whose known defects are already zero. Each new commit lands into a repository whose earlier defects were caught, ledgered, and turned into rules the system enforces next time. Removed rework compounds. Compounded removed rework is the productivity multiplier.

The public number worth naming, again, is a productivity multiplier of three to five times in year one and ten to twenty times in year two for teams that adopt the whole model, not one primitive at a time. Year one gains come from the jury and RADAR. Year two gains come from the Convergence Loop as the failure ledger hardens. Teams that adopt only one primitive get a fraction of the multiplier and often conclude, incorrectly, that the model does not work.

A closing scope reminder. ADM answers a fourth question at the runtime layer, which is what a coworking agent is authorized to attempt in the first place and how the runtime refuses actions outside that authorization even when credentials would technically permit them. That is a security-architecture topic, and it will be the subject of a dedicated essay rather than a compressed section in the coworking series. Subscribe if you want it in your inbox when it lands.

Agile did not fail. Its contract expired. The Agentic Development Model is the replacement contract for coworking teams, and this essay is the manual. If it resonated, use it. If it did not, tell me why. The model is still improving, and it improves fastest against real feedback from teams running it.
