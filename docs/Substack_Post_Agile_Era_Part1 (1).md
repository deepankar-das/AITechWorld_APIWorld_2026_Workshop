# The Post-Agile Era: Building with Co-Working Humans and Agents

### Part 1 of a three-part series. Agile didn't fail. Its contract expired.

*By Deepankar Das | deepankardas.com*

*Thanks for reading. Subscribe for free to receive Part 2, Part 3, and future essays directly in your inbox.*

---

In May 2026, four of the largest technology companies on earth, Uber, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta, told their engineers, in writing and in some cases through performance reviews, that AI tool usage was now a condition of employment. Inside three of those companies, the AI budget burned through before the fiscal year ended. Inside the fourth, engineers built leaderboards to game the metric until the leaderboards themselves had to be taken down. In late June, Ford confirmed it had rehired roughly three hundred and fifty veteran quality inspectors after an AI-only inspection program failed to hold the line on quality. In July, Tesla capped every employee at two hundred dollars a week of AI spend after internal dashboards revealed engineers ranking each other by token consumption, thousands of dollars a week per person. The pattern in two months was already unmistakable: mandate at the top, gaming in the middle, retreat at the bottom.

Three weeks later, on June 11, 2026, Gartner published a finding that should have rearranged every roadmap in the industry: **40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by 2027\.** Cancelled. Not delayed. Not de-scoped. Cancelled.

Put those two facts next to each other. The mandate from the top says: deliver 10x. The execution data from the floor says: four out of ten of you will not deliver anything at all.

This is the defining problem of enterprise software in 2026\. It is not a tooling problem. It is not a model-capability problem. It is not a prompt-engineering problem. The tools shipped. The models shipped. The teams that are stuck are still stuck. The gap between the mandate and the delivery is not a gap that better technology will close.

The gap is methodological. And the methodology we have been running for twenty years, Agile, in all of its variations, was not built to close it.

Ford's fleet did not fail because the vehicles were bad. It failed because nobody had a model for building with autonomous systems as coworkers rather than as tools. That is the frame this essay operates in. What we are living through is not a tooling upgrade. It is a substrate change in the team itself. The team is no longer humans plus tools. It is humans and agents as coworkers, and every ceremony and every primitive Agile was built on assumed the team was humans only.

## What the Data Shows

The gap is methodological, not technical, and a wide range of evidence from multiple independent sources is now converging on the same conclusion.

**The tools shipped, and the trust did not follow.** Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey, drawn from 49,000 respondents across 177 countries, reports AI coding tool adoption at 84%, a record high. In the same survey, developer trust in AI output has fallen from 40% in 2024 to 29% in 2025\. Only 3% of respondents "highly trust" the code these tools produce. The top frustration, cited by 66% of developers, is "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite," and 45% say debugging AI-generated code takes longer than writing it manually. Adoption is at infrastructure levels. Trust is at an all-time low. The tools are not the blocker.

**Code volume is up, and downstream quality is collapsing.** Faros AI's 2026 Engineering Report, based on two years of telemetry from 22,000 developers across 4,000 teams, found that as the same organizations moved from low to high AI adoption, task throughput per developer rose 33.7%, epics completed per developer rose 66.2%, and PR merge rate per developer rose 16.2%. At the same time, bugs per developer rose 54%, incidents per merged PR rose 242.7%, code churn rose 861%, median PR review time rose 441.5%, and 31.3% more code reached production with no review at all. The Faros authors call this the Acceleration Whiplash, and they offer one line that captures it: "Throughput measures what was shipped, not what survived." Their finding that strong pre-AI engineering maturity provides no protection from this pattern is the most important sentence in the report.

**The Agile community is documenting the same failure from the other side.** The 18th State of Agile Report (2025) found that 63% of organizations struggle to deliver reliable, high-quality software, up twelve points year over year, while only 27% say Agile is actually enabling them to deliver value. Only 13% describe Agile as deeply embedded in their business. The report's own language is blunt: "Technical teams are optimising for metrics that don't matter to the business." The methodology's own annual self-assessment is documenting that the methodology has decoupled from business outcomes.

**The analyst houses have converged on the same prescription.** Forrester's *State of Agentic AI 2026*, published in June 2026, prescribes the fix in a single sentence: "Redesign the work, not just the tooling. Agents bolted onto human-paced legacy workflows produce task savings, not step-change value." MIT NANDA's *GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025*, based on 150 executive interviews and 300 AI projects, found that 95% of GenAI pilots delivered no measurable P\&L impact, and the failure pattern there is not capability, it is integration.

When the developer survey, the telemetry study, the Agile community's own report, and two major analyst houses all converge on the same diagnosis, the diagnosis is no longer controversial. The methodology is the bottleneck. The replacement is the work. This essay is about the shape of that replacement. Part 2, next week, walks through the model itself.

I want to be careful about a thing before I begin. This is not an obituary for Agile. Agile worked. It worked because the contract it offered, short iterations, human-sized stories, velocity measured in human throughput, a backlog ordered by product manager judgment, matched the constraints of the era it was designed for. That contract has expired. The constraints have moved. Saying so is not the same as saying Agile failed. It is saying the world it was designed for is gone.

## What Actually Broke

For thirty years, software economics put compute, talent, and time on the scarce side of the ledger, and ambition on the abundant side. Every engineer I know grew up rationing the left column to feed the right column. Sprints were built around this. Stories were sized to a human's day because the day was the bottleneck. Velocity was measured in points per human because the human was the constraint. Backlogs were ordered by product manager judgment alone because the cost of executing the wrong item was high enough to demand a human gatekeeper.

In 2026, that flipped. Compute is abundant. Tokens are abundant. Agent hours are abundant. Agents do not get tired, do not take PTO, do not require ramp-up time on a new codebase. Code generation, at sufficient quality for a wide class of work, is abundant.

What is scarce now is on the other side of the ledger.

**Ambition is scarce.** Most organizations cannot articulate, at the level of detail and velocity required, what they want and how much they want their AI investment to produce. Delivery has to happen at scale for the tokens spent on customer problems to matter and to count toward ROI. The ambition itself has to reset. If the same team now produces at 5x to 10x its prior throughput, management cannot keep asking for the same roadmap on the same calendar. The mandate has to become a larger roadmap delivered in one-fifth to one-tenth the time, with the same headcount. Anything less than that leaves the multiplier on the table, and the CFO will find it before the CTO does.

**Direction is scarce.** Most senior executives cannot point the arrow at a problem large enough to absorb what their newly-multiplied workforce can produce. When agents make a team 10x more productive on their current scope, the strategic question is no longer how to do more of what the team already does. It is what 10x larger problem the team should now be solving. Most executive teams do not have a credible answer to that question, and the absence of an answer is the single most expensive failure in enterprise AI today. When the arrow is not pointed at a problem worthy of the new output, the organization runs the multiplier against the same problem it was already solving, and produces only excess capacity, which the CFO then converts to layoffs. The 40% cancellation rate is not, in most cases, a failure of execution. It is a failure of direction, papered over with execution metrics. Ford rehiring three hundred and fifty inspectors in late June and Tesla capping engineer AI spend in July are the same story told from two industries: the multiplier was pointed at the wrong problem, so the organization retreated rather than continuing to fund the pointing.

**Real-world, grounded, and recent organization-specific context is scarce.** AI coding assistants answer every question with the same confident tone whether they know the answer or not. That confidence is contagious. The engineer using the assistant stops asking the questions that not-knowing used to force: what does the actual customer do with this workflow today, what did the last three stakeholder conversations reveal that never made it into the ticket, how is this deployed in production, what does the ops team already know about how it fails, what did the last two incidents teach us about this subsystem, and, at least as important, how does this specific company already do this. The assistant will happily fill all of those gaps with fluent-sounding output, and the engineer, feeling productive, ships a feature that looks production-worthy but was built on hallucinated context instead of real context.

Four distinct kinds of context are at stake, and all four are scarce.

**Real-world context** is the operational, deployment, and customer-behavior facts that only exist outside the codebase.

**Grounded context** is the specific, verified information the model has actually been given and can point to, as opposed to the plausible output it will generate when it has not been given anything at all.

**Recent organization-specific context** is the live input from other functions and stakeholders that only exists inside the heads of specific people this week. What product actually wants this quarter, which is different from what they wanted last quarter. What sales heard on the last three enterprise calls that never made it into a ticket. What customer success is seeing in the last two weeks of support conversations. What finance or pricing just decided about how this feature should be monetized. What compliance or legal just ruled on this specific case, as opposed to the general policy. What the ops team learned in the last incident that has not been written up yet. What the design partner or beta customer said in yesterday's call. This is the input the engineer used to go and get every time, because the answer is different every time. Documented artifacts, prior architectural decisions, general policies, and settled naming conventions do not belong in this category. A well-instrumented assistant can be given all of that, and should be. It cannot be given a conversation that has not happened yet, or an intention that has not been written down.

**System-lifecycle context** is the accreted history of an existing application that is being modernized: the prior deployments and the architectural decisions taken along the way, the runtime and infrastructure the system runs on today, the stakeholder input that shaped what the system was built to do, and the partner and integration-partner input that governs how it talks to everything upstream and downstream of it. Most enterprise AI work in 2026 is not greenfield. It is brownfield modernization of systems that have been running for a decade or more, and it is the modality where AI assistance underperforms the most.

The industry has already named this the greenfield-versus-brownfield gap, and the data is stark. Stanford's 2025 study of 100,000+ developers across 600+ companies found AI productivity gains of 30 to 40% on greenfield low-complexity work, dropping to 15 to 20% on brownfield low-complexity work and to 0 to 10%, sometimes negative, on brownfield high-complexity work. The [SWE-EVO benchmark](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-19-ai-coding-agents-legacy-codebases), which measures long-horizon software evolution on real legacy systems, shows the best current models resolving 21% of tasks compared to 65% on the greenfield-flavored SWE-bench Verified. That 21% versus 65% gap is the industrial cost of missing lifecycle context.

Practitioners have started calling this [the Archaeology Problem](https://www.driver.ai/blog/the-archaeology-problem-why-ai-coding-agents-fail-on-legacy-codebases): AI coding agents at enterprise scale are not unintelligent, they are blind, because the real constraints on what a function can safely do are scattered across incident postmortems from 2019, a Slack thread from 2021, deployment runbooks nobody updated, and the institutional memory of a developer who left the company three years ago, none of which exists in a file the agent can read. Gartner's D\&A Summit 2026 opening keynote declared context [the new critical infrastructure](https://atlan.com/know/gartner/key-takeaways-from-gartner-da-summit-2026/) for exactly this reason: four out of five organizations increased AI investments in 2026, and only one in five is showing measurable ROI, and the gap is fragmented context scattered across documentation, tribal knowledge, and disconnected tools.

The lesson every modernization team is now learning the hard way is that an AI assistant can be given the code and still not be given the system, because the system is more than the code. Prior deployments, current environment, stakeholder history, and partner integrations are each a distinct layer of the system, each was decided by humans over years, and each has to be reconstructed for the Human plus AI team before the AI half of that team can safely change anything material.

Some AI practitioners assume that connecting the assistant to Slack, the ticket system, the wiki, the databases, and the other systems where this history lives is enough. It is not. Raw connectors give the model reach, not comprehension. Slack threads, incident reports, deployment logs, and prior architectural decisions have to be extracted, deduplicated, disambiguated, tied back to the specific subsystem they constrain, and presented to the model in a structured form that is both retrievable at decision time and actionable at the point of code generation. Structuring the history is the work. Connecting to the sources is only the first step of that work, and treating the connection as if it were the whole answer is one of the quieter failure modes in enterprise AI adoption today.

Recent-organization-specific context is missing because nobody has had that conversation yet this week. System-lifecycle context is missing because the conversations that shaped this system happened years ago, with people who are no longer in the room, and the artifacts they left behind capture the outcome but not the intent. Both scarcities produce the same visible failure, which is plausible output that ships on top of missing context, but they demand different remedies. Recent-organization-specific context is a forcing-function problem, solvable by putting stakeholders back in the conversation the assistant was allowed to skip. System-lifecycle context is an excavation problem, solvable only by treating the reconstruction of prior deployments, current environment, and stakeholder-and-partner history as a first-class deliverable the modernization program funds, staffs, and gates on.

It is worth stopping on the obvious question here, because the industry keeps stepping past it. Does anyone actually believe that everything relevant to a live application has been documented, and that nothing material is left out of the product manager's head, the architect's head, the solution engineer's head, the deployment scripts, the customer's actual workflow, the incident postmortems, the deals-desk exceptions, the compliance decisions from last quarter, or the ops runbooks that never got written down? Nobody believes this. Every practitioner in the field will admit it in private. And yet the mental model most teams operate under assumes the opposite, because it is the only assumption under which "just point the agent at the repo" produces trustworthy output.

If everything is not documented, then not everything is available to any LLM. Not in its training data, because the training data is public and generic. Not in the repository, because the repository is code, not the reasons the code is the way it is. Not in the wiki, because the wiki is what somebody had time to write and remembered to update. The parts that decide whether a change is safe or unsafe, whether a feature will land or backfire, whether an integration will hold or break, live in places no crawler can reach: the solution engineer's memory of what the customer actually asked for, the on-call's memory of the last outage, the account executive's memory of what was promised on the last three enterprise calls, the tribal knowledge of "we do not do X because it broke prod in 2023," and the deployment quirks that are not in any script because the person who works around them has been working around them for so long it does not occur to them to write it down.

That is what puts the human coworker upstream of the agent, not just alongside it. Somebody has to craft the missing context from their own experience, and elicit the rest through conversations with the people who hold it: product, sales, customer success, on-call, ops, compliance, design partners, the tenured engineer who remembers why the schema is the way it is. Somebody has to assemble that into a complete-enough package and hand it to the coding agent as declared, grounded input. Without that step, the agent is not building against the application; it is building against a plausibly-shaped hallucination of the application, and every guardrail downstream is doing pattern-match cleanup on work that was under-specified from the start. The guardrails catch bad output. The context-craft step prevents the class of bad output that comes from the agent not knowing something nobody wrote down.

The old friction of not-knowing was a forcing function. It sent engineers to talk to product, to sales, to customer success, to the operators running the last release, to the actual customers who use the workflow every day, and to the tenured engineers who remember why the system was built the way it was. Removing that friction without replacing the forcing function is one of the quietest failure modes in the entire agentic development stack, because the code compiles, the tests the agent wrote pass, and nothing about the artifact signals that the context underneath it was fabricated or generically substituted.

**Specification matching the delivery velocity of the AI-enabled team is scarce.** The artifacts that turn a now expanded ambition into something an agent can execute against, the expanded specs, the acceptance criteria, the blast-radius assessments, do not exist in most engineering organizations as a discipline. Sizing those specs to what the AI-enabled team can actually deliver is what turns delivery into ROI, and no team writing three-bullet tickets is producing specifications at that resolution.

**Organizational alignment is scarce.** Each function feels AI differently. Marketing feels it as content velocity and campaign personalization. Engineering feels it as code generation, review load, and pull-request throughput. The CEO feels it as a board narrative and a competitive-threat clock. Finance feels it as a token bill and a headcount question. Each of them would write down a different definition of what AI success looks like, and the organization has no shared outcome to align them against, which is why "aligned around AI" is a phrase most companies use but very few can point at.

**A measurable outcome for AI adoption is scarce.** Because a shared outcome has not been articulated, leadership defaults to a measurable input, and the input it typically reaches for is volume of tokens spent per engineer per week. Volume of consumption is exactly the wrong metric, because it rewards the engineer who spent the most and penalizes the engineer who solved the problem in one well-shaped prompt. The right metric is the inverse: highest delivered value or ROI produced with the fewest tokens used. That number is harder to compute, which is precisely why leadership does not yet reach for it, and why a token-volume mandate keeps getting installed as the visible proxy and keeps getting gamed exactly as it was designed to be.

**Judgment is scarce.** When agents can generate twenty plausible implementations of a feature in an afternoon, the bottleneck is no longer producing options, it is selecting from them.

**Review capacity is scarce.** Agents now generate faster than any team of humans can read.

**A model for humans and agents to co-work is scarce, the scarcity that contains all the others.** Every organization in the industry is now operating with humans and agents in the same workflow. Almost none of them have a model that describes how the two should co-work.

What they have instead is humans following a methodology designed for humans, and agents bolted onto the side of it. They have agents generating code, browsing the web, drafting specs, running tests, and shipping artifacts, inside an operating model that does not acknowledge the agents exist.

The result is a workforce in which the work has never been demarcated between the human and the agent, accountability defaults to the human by inheritance rather than by design ([Springvanta](https://springvanta.com/blog/2-4m-autonomous-agents-7-percent-accountability-three-reports-june-2026) reports that only 7% of organizations have named a single accountable individual for agent behavior), the human does not have full knowledge of what the agent actually did between commits, and the human cannot operate at the speed at which the agent is producing work in the first place ([Esteban Sancho](https://estebansancho.com/blog/2026/01/24/why-code-review-breaks-at-ai-velocity.html) estimates that a 2,000-line AI-generated pull request needs five to ten hours to review effectively, and most reviews get thirty minutes).

The org chart still says everything reports to humans even though machine throughput is now an order of magnitude larger than human throughput on the same team, and the decisions embedded in that throughput are being made at the same order-of-magnitude gap ahead of the humans in the reporting line who are supposed to be reviewing them.

A model for humans and agents to co-work has to specify, with the same rigor Agile once specified human-only workflows, who does what when humans and agents work together:

- Which work humans originate and which work agents originate  
- Which work humans review and which work agents pre-review  
- Which decisions humans must own and which can be delegated to agents under guardrails  
- How cost is budgeted when one of the coworkers is a meter that runs every second  
- How throughput is measured when one of the coworkers does not sleep  
- How trust is earned and lost between the two classes of coworker  
- How the org chart reorganizes around the conductor-and-orchestra structure that this work actually has

That model does not exist as a default in any organization in 2026\. Until it does, every other scarcity on this list will keep showing up, because the missing operating model is the thing that would have allocated ambition, direction, specification, alignment, judgment, and review across the humans and agents that should each be carrying their part of it. This is the scarcity nobody is funding. It is also the only one whose absence guarantees the failure of all the others. I have spent the last two years building and advising on this operating model. The rest of this essay refers to it as the Agentic Development Model, or ADM. Part 2 walks through it in full.

Every clause of the Agile contract assumed a scarcity distribution that has now landed on the wrong side of the ledger with the advent of humans and AI agents as coworkers. Pick any one of them. Two-week sprints made sense when humans were the bottleneck, they do not make sense when the inner iteration runs in minutes and the outer iteration in months. Stories sized to a human's day made sense when the human was the unit of production, they do not make sense when the unit is an agent's context window. Velocity measured in points per human told you something useful when human throughput was the constraint, it tells you almost nothing now.

Agile is not wrong. Agile is being asked to do a job it was not designed to do, in conditions it was not designed for, against constraints it does not recognize.

## What Happens When You Bolt AI Onto Agile

Six failure modes are running, right now, in real engineering organizations that have not changed their operating model. I have watched all six play out in companies I have advised or observed. Each one shows up in the field with such regularity that they deserve names, and each one is the visible field manifestation of one of the scarcities named in the previous section.

### Ambition Freeze

This is the failure mode that sits earliest in the chain, and the one most companies do not realize they are running. The CEO issued the AI mandate. Tools were rolled out. Engineers started using agents. Throughput went up. And then the roadmap, the calendar, and the quarterly targets did not move. The team is now capable of shipping five to ten times what it shipped a year ago, and it is being asked to ship the same thing on the same timeline. The multiplier the organization just paid for is sitting on the floor, unclaimed. Engineers notice first. They finish a week's work by Wednesday and slow down for the rest of the week, because there is no expanded ambition telling them what to do with the extra capacity. The mandate was to use AI. The mandate was never to become an organization that could deliver a larger roadmap in one-fifth the time with the same headcount. Without that second mandate, the first one produces idle capacity dressed up as productivity, and idle capacity is what the CFO eventually finds a use for, in ways the CTO will not enjoy.

### Token Maxxing

This is the failure mode I have written about most, in [The Token Maxing Trap](https://deepankardas.substack.com/p/the-token-maxing-trap-why-forcing) in May 2026, and the one that produces the most vivid artifacts. When a CEO mandates AI adoption and the organization measures adoption by token consumption, employees do exactly what the incentive structure rewards. They run agents at full throttle on work nobody asked for. They burn compute to look busy. The Uber, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta stories from May 2026 are not isolated incidents. They are the predictable output of measuring inputs instead of outcomes.

By July 2026, the pattern had produced its clearest artifact yet. Tesla capped employee AI spending at two hundred dollars a week, effective July 6, after The Information reported that software engineers had been consuming thousands of dollars of tokens each week and that Tesla teams had built internal dashboards ranking employees by token consumption. A literal tokenmaxxing leaderboard, running inside the company, treated as normal until the finance function noticed. Meta had already been through the same cycle in April, when [Fortune reported](https://fortune.com/2026/04/09/meta-killed-employee-ai-token-dashboard/) that an anonymous employee had built a Claudeonomics dashboard tracking eighty-five thousand Meta engineers by token spend, complete with titles like "Token Legend" and "Cache Wizard" for the top consumers. The top individual on that leaderboard consumed two hundred and eighty-one billion tokens in thirty days, roughly one point four million dollars of Anthropic spend by one person in a month. Meta shut the dashboard down two days after the story broke. Tesla went the other direction and imposed the cap. Uber landed at fifteen hundred dollars a month per engineer after burning its 2026 AI budget by April. Microsoft engineers were reported spending five hundred to two thousand dollars a month on Claude Code tokens.

Agile's sprint cadence makes this worse, not better, because the sprint creates pressure to demonstrate progress at fixed intervals, and tokens consumed is a metric the team can move on demand. The leaderboards are not the disease. The leaderboards are the symptom of an operating model that had no way to distinguish token spend tied to a customer outcome from token spend tied to a performance review. Every organization on this list is now discovering the same lesson in the same order: measure inputs and you will get inputs, measure outcomes and you have to build the operating model that can specify them.

### Inadequate Specifications

This is the failure mode that hits engineering teams hardest and the one most senior engineers recognize the moment it is named. The specifications the team is writing today were sized for a world in which humans were going to interpret and execute them. A ticket at the top of the sprint was three or four bullet points, the acceptance criteria fit in a paragraph, and the blast radius was left to the engineer's judgment because the engineer was a person who could ask. Those specifications do not account for the full velocity of an AI-enabled team. Agents burn through a week of that kind of work in an afternoon and then sit idle at the bottom of the pipeline, waiting for the next well-defined thing. The team that felt like it had a two-sprint backlog on Monday morning has an empty backlog by Wednesday afternoon, and the specification writers are still on the human cadence, planning the next sprint the way they always did. The specs that do land are the old ones, at the old size and the old level of detail, and the agents fill in the missing acceptance criteria with plausible guesses instead of the customer conversations that would have produced the real ones. The visible symptom is agents idling. The actual symptom is inadequate specifications shipping as if they were adequate. Everything downstream, including review, inherits the gap.

### Demo Theater

This failure mode looks different on the surface from Ambition Freeze and Token Maxxing but shares a root cause with both. Engineering teams produce impressive prototypes during sprint reviews. The CEO is thrilled. Screen recordings circulate on LinkedIn. Six months later, no customer has used the thing. The agents shipped capability; nobody shipped value. The reason is structural: most organizations reward the increment, not the outcome. An increment that looks like a working feature is celebrated the same as an increment that actually moves a business metric. When agents make it dramatically cheaper to produce impressive-looking increments, the gap between increment and outcome widens faster than anyone notices.

### Review Collapse

This is the failure mode that kills teams. Agents generate code faster than humans can review it. The team starts cutting corners on review to keep up. Grave defects ship. Customers complain. The team's trust in the agents erodes. The agents get turned off, or restricted to such a small surface area that they produce no leverage. Six months and several hundred thousand dollars of compute later, the team is back where it started, minus six months, minus the compute spend, minus the engineers who left because they were tired of cleaning up broken builds. I have seen this pattern hit in week six to week ten of an agentic rollout with such reliability that I can almost mark it on a calendar.

In late June 2026, Ford confirmed the industrial-scale version of the same failure. The company had bet that AI-driven quality inspection could substitute for veteran human inspectors on the line. Charles Poon, Ford's vice president of vehicle hardware engineering, said the quiet part out loud: "Mistakenly, we thought that by just introducing artificial intelligence and ingesting the design requirements that we had, that would produce a high-quality product." Ford quietly rehired roughly three hundred and fifty of those veteran inspectors. Nine hundred AI-powered cameras still run on the line, but they run alongside the humans, not instead of them. The result of the reversal was that Ford became the top-ranked mainstream automaker in the J.D. Power 2026 Initial Quality Study, its first time in that position since 2010\. The lesson Ford paid for at automotive-recall scale is the same lesson software teams pay for at incident-report scale. The AI shipped the throughput. The humans shipped the trust. Removing the humans removed the trust. Adding them back restored it. Review Collapse is not a software-specific pathology. It is what happens in any production system when the review layer gets sized against the old throughput. Reviewers inherit the same false confidence the author absorbed from the assistant. A diff that reads cleanly, with tests that pass and a description written in the assistant's fluent register, does not surface the missing stakeholder conversation, the missing customer input, the missing deployment reality, or the missing this-week decision from product, sales, ops, or compliance that would have made the reviewer stop and ask. The absence of that live, organization-specific context is invisible on the surface of the artifact, which is precisely why grave defects ship.

The Faros 2026 telemetry now puts hard numbers behind what was once an anecdotal pattern. Median PR review time is up 441.5%, with 31.3% more code merging with no human or agent review at all. Code churn, the volume of code that gets written and then deleted, is up 861%. Incidents per merged PR are up 242.7%. These are the fingerprints of Review Collapse, captured in industry-scale telemetry across 22,000 developers in the same organizations comparing their own low-AI quarters to their high-AI quarters. Faros's finding that strong pre-AI engineering maturity provides no protection from this pattern is the part that should disturb every CTO reading this essay. The teams that did everything right by 2024 standards are hitting the same wall as the teams that did not. The wall is structural. It is what the existing operating model produces when AI velocity is applied on top of human-paced gates.

### Agile Primitives Outrun

The sixth failure mode is the one that will be embarrassing in retrospect, because every symptom of it is visible today and almost nobody has named it. The primitives Agile was built on have been outrun by AI-enabled velocity, and the industry has been running for two years without coining the vocabulary that would replace them. The tools that encoded those primitives were the visible casualty; the deeper casualty is that the underlying unit of measurement no longer measures the work.

Start with the primitive itself. A story point was, in most teams' informal practice, roughly half a human day to a full human day of work. [SAFe's own initial-capacity guidance](https://scaledagile.com/) sets it explicitly at half a day of development and half a day of test and validation, which is one working day per point when the team has no historical velocity data yet. When an agent executes that same one-point story in fifteen minutes and then sits idle waiting for the next one, the primitive is no longer the right resolution. It is too large by a factor of sixteen to thirty-two, depending on which end of the practitioner range the team's original scale was calibrated to. Half a story point is not the answer either, because the primitive was never quantitative to begin with, and dividing a vibe does not produce a smaller vibe. The team can drop story points entirely, and many are quietly doing so, but nothing has taken their place. There is no widely accepted name for the AI-era analog. Tokens are a spend metric, not a work-size metric. Agent-minutes vary by model and are not comparable across a fleet. Spec-units have not been defined. The primitive has been outrun and no substitute has been coined, so teams are estimating in a vocabulary that no longer applies to the thing being estimated.

The ceremonies inherit the same problem, and the sprint review shows it most clearly. A two-week sprint demo was calibrated to what a human-paced team could ship in two weeks, which was typically one or two feature-sized increments the team could deep-dive into with the product owner and any stakeholders in the room. An AI-enabled team executing at five to ten times that velocity is now bringing ten to twenty feature-sized increments to the same review. The fifteen-minute demo becomes a much longer walkthrough of a substantially larger increment, or it becomes a highlight reel that hides most of the actual work under the fold, and neither option is what the sprint review was designed to do. Planning poker has the same shape of failure. Sitting seven engineers in a room to estimate a spec the agents will burn through by lunch is theater. Backlog grooming as a weekly hour-long ceremony cannot refill a pipeline that execution empties in an afternoon. Every one of these ceremonies was sized against a tempo the team no longer runs at, and shortening or lengthening them does not fix the mismatch. The primitive underneath each ceremony is what needs to change, and no replacement primitive has been agreed on.

The tools were the visible casualty of all of this, and the displacement is the part that will make the news first. Jira, VersionOne, Linear, Azure DevOps, Asana, Monday, and Rally, the entire multi-billion-dollar category of enterprise Agile-planning software, have been quietly displaced in the operating models that are running agents at scale. Reporting from June 2026 documents the pattern directly: engineering teams are shipping [git-native governance layers where plans, decisions, and work items live as plain markdown in the repo with no database, no dashboard, and no vendor](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinbellero_an-ai-agent-probably-wrote-code-in-your-repo-activity-7470943098538364930-v7JP), and practitioners are publicly describing operating models that [replace Scrum, Jira, and the team wiki with agents coordinating through repo markdown](https://dev.to/mickyarun/i-replaced-scrum-jira-and-our-wiki-with-12-ai-agents-on-a-mac-mini-o7o). The team's implementation plan is not in Jira. It is a markdown or HTML file in the repo, sitting next to the code, which the agents read directly and update in place as they execute. The specification the agent works against is another file in the repo. The acceptance criteria live in the same file. The task list, the ownership assignments, the dependency graph, and the parallel-execution routing across the fleet of agents are all in the repo, in files the agents wrote and the agents consume. Task distribution, task assignment, and parallel execution across multiple agents are now handled by the AI coding agents themselves, coordinating through the plan file rather than through a ticket queue that a human would tend to. The Jira dashboard that engineering leadership still opens every morning is looking at a projection of yesterday's ceremony, populated by an integration that reads the repo and manufactures the ticket state after the fact, not at the artifact where the work actually lives.

The vendor tools were not displaced only because they were slow or ceremony-heavy. They were displaced because their data model has no schema for either half of the ratio the previous section named as the right metric for AI adoption. You cannot compute value-per-token from Jira, because Jira has no field for tokens spent and no field for outcome delivered. Story points is what Jira stores, and story points no longer measure the work. A markdown implementation-plan file can carry both halves of the ratio, because it can record the customer outcome the spec targets and the token spend the agents logged in the plan as they executed against it. Value-per-token is computable from those files. It is not computable from the vendor tool. That is the second reason the workflow migrated into the repo, and it is the reason the migration is not reversing. The vocabulary broke. The tools that reported on the broken vocabulary broke with it. The replacement vocabulary has not yet been written, which is why every organization I have watched adopt agents at scale is running, right now, on a wiki-and-repo-based operating model that has no name, no vendor, no license, and no dashboard the CEO can open in a board meeting.

The 40% cancellation figure that Gartner published lives inside these six failure modes. Every cancelled project I have looked at, post-mortem, was killed by some combination of ambition freeze, token maxxing, inadequate specifications, demo theater, review collapse, and Agile primitives outrun, never by an agent that did not work, and rarely by a model that was not capable enough.

## What Moves Trust

The variable underneath all six failure modes is trust. Ambition Freeze is what teams produce when leadership does not yet trust the multiplier enough to reset the roadmap against it. Token Maxxing is what teams produce when nobody trusts the output enough to slow down and verify it, so they optimize the input the organization can see instead. Inadequate Specifications are what get written when the specification layer does not yet trust the AI-enabled tempo it is now supposed to feed. Demo Theater is what executives accept when they cannot tell the difference between something that works and something that looks like it works. Review Collapse is what happens when the trust between humans and agents breaks down and never gets rebuilt. Agile Primitives Outrun is what happens when the vocabulary the organization used to reason about work stops describing the work, and the tools that reported on that vocabulary quietly stop being where the work lives. Trust is the thing being burned. Any replacement contract that does not move trust deliberately is a replacement in name only.

The question worth asking, then, is what actually moves trust. Engineers do not trust code because a model produced it. Engineers trust code they can verify against an intent they understand, that has been checked against acceptance criteria they helped write, that carries an audit trail back to a spec, and that has been put through a Convergence Loop which drove the residual defects to zero. Trust is not earned by the output. It is earned by the inputs and the gates the output had to pass through.

The 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey cited earlier in this essay, drawn from 49,000 respondents across 177 countries, tells you which inputs are missing today. The single largest developer frustration in that survey, cited by 66% of respondents, is code from AI tools that is "almost right, but not quite." That description is exactly what code looks like when there is no declared specification to check it against, no acceptance criteria to verify it against, and no Convergence Loop to close the residual gap. The agent shipped something plausible. The engineer cannot tell whether it is correct without reading every line. Reading every line is exactly what humans cannot do at agent velocity. So trust falls, adoption rises anyway because the mandate from the top demands it, and the gap between adoption and trust is where the 40% cancellations live.

The replacement contract has to move trust up, deliberately, by changing the inputs and the gates. Specs are written before any agent runs, so the engineer has a declared intent to verify against. Intent is pinned at every layer of the system and gates every action the agent takes, so the agent cannot drift from what was asked. A convergence phase drives residual defects to zero before the diff ever reaches human review, so what the human reviewer sees is small, focused, and worth their attention. Guardrails are explicit and named, so the engineer knows what the agent was prevented from doing. The audit log makes every decision traceable back to the spec it served. None of these are individually novel. The argument is that trust does not move until all of them are in place together, and that the operating model has to require all of them at once. Trust is a system property, not a tool property. The replacement contract has to specify the system.

## Introducing the Agentic Development Model

The Agentic Development Model, ADM for short, is what I have been building and advising on for the last two years as the replacement contract. It rests on three Tier 1 primitives that arrange themselves in the causal order a single diff actually flows through them, and each one answers one class of failure the previous section named.

**Cross-Model Convergence Review** is a three-member LLM jury drawn from three different foundational-model families that peer-reviews each agent-authored diff and converges the divergent findings into a single owned action list. This is the primitive that answers Review Collapse without asking humans to read every diff. Each model family carries a distinct blind-spot profile, and the research on why cross-family ensembling captures roughly 95% of the theoretical ceiling of a diversity-preserving jury has firmed up quickly ([Wisdom and Delusion of LLM Ensembles for Code, arXiv 2510.21513](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21513v1)). Part 2 walks through the defect taxonomy each juror surfaces and the cross-model ownership graph the jurors produce.

**RADAR**, Risk-Aware Dependency Analysis for Rapid Verification, is the test-selection paradigm the operating model uses to keep the pipeline economically viable at agent velocity. Coverage as a metric is dead at agent velocity because full-suite regressions on every agent-authored diff are neither fast enough nor cheap enough to sustain, and skipping tests is what produces the bad-quality exposure that surfaces in production. RADAR selects tests on multi-dimensional blast radius, binds each test to a false-negative-rate target, and enforces mandatory floors for critical invariants that no selector can undercut. Part 2 walks through the full mechanism.

**The Convergence Loop** is the process primitive that takes what the jury found and what RADAR ran against the diff and drives both to zero, iteration by iteration, before the diff ever reaches human review. It sits at the center of ADM, and it is the primitive that finally makes left-shift real rather than aspirational. Left-shift has been the stated goal of every quality movement for two decades and has never worked at scale, because the loop always terminated on a schedule, not on a quality gate. The Convergence Loop inverts that: it terminates when residual defects reach zero, not when the calendar says so. It runs eight steps in order, and both product code and test code are first-class citizens throughout. Part 2 walks through all eight.

Those three primitives compose into an end-to-end operating model. The jury reviews the diff. RADAR selects the risk-weighted tests. The Convergence Loop drives residual defects to zero. The commit-attested gate admits only diffs whose receipts prove the loop terminated cleanly on that specific change. Above the diff-level pipeline sits a delivery dashboard that ties per-diff operations to overall project progress and gives leadership the CEO-openable view the vendor tools currently hallucinate. The dashboard is not itself a Tier 1 primitive. It is the artifact that makes ADM legible to the org above the engineering line.

A note on runtime authorization. This essay is scoped to how humans and agents coword on shipping code. There is a separate, load-bearing question ADM addresses at the runtime layer: what a coworking agent is authorized to attempt in the first place, and how the runtime refuses actions outside that authorization even when credentials would technically permit them. That question deserves its own essay rather than a compressed section here, and it will get one.

The substrate for this vocabulary, meaning plans, ownership, and coordination as repo-resident markdown, already exists in the wild, as Justin Bellero and Mickyarun documented in their June 2026 write-ups. ADM's contribution is the operating model that sits on top of that substrate: the three Tier 1 primitives, the ceremonies that bind to those primitives rather than to a two-week clock, the failure ledger, the two-state closure, the value-per-token ratio, and the canonical file templates that give the substrate a governable structure.

## What ADM Buys You

The composition of the three primitives is what earns the productivity multiplier. Each primitive removes one class of rework: rework the jury catches before it ships, rework RADAR catches at the risk-weighted subset that matters, and rework the Convergence Loop catches before human review. Removed rework is the compounding lever. It compounds because each iteration of the loop starts from a diff whose known defects are already zero, and each new commit lands into a repository whose earlier defects were caught, ledgered, and turned into rules the system enforces on the next run.

The public number worth naming is a productivity multiplier of three to five times in year one and ten to twenty times in year two for teams that adopt the whole model, not one primitive at a time. Year one gains come from the jury and RADAR: less human review time per diff, and less full-suite regression cost per commit. Year two gains come from the Convergence Loop: less rework across the pipeline as the failure ledger hardens and residual defects converge to zero before the diff reaches human review.

The 40% cancellation figure that Gartner published lives on the other side of that multiplier. Every cancelled project I have looked at, post-mortem, was killed by some combination of the six failure modes named above, never by an agent that did not work, and rarely by a model that was not capable enough. ADM is the operating model designed to catch each of those failure modes at the primitive level rather than at the retrospective.

## The Bottom Line for Part 1

The post-Agile era is not a future condition. It is the operating condition of every engineering organization that has taken the AI mandate seriously, whether they have named it or not. The question is no longer whether the operating model needs to change. The question is whether your organization will change it on purpose, with a coherent replacement, or change it under duress, in a series of cancelled projects and quiet layoffs.

Tools changed in 2023\. Models changed in 2024\. The execution model has to change in 2026\. That is the work of the next twelve months, and it is the work that decides which side of the 40% line a given organization ends up on.

Agile did not fail. Its contract expired. The replacement is being built right now, in essays like this one and in keynotes and in the first wave of engineering organizations that have stopped pretending the old contract still works. The companies that get there first, that move on purpose, with a real operating model, built around co-working humans and agents, governed by guardrails, and disciplined by convergence, are the ones that will compound through the rest of the decade.

The mandate without the execution model produces only frustration. The execution model without the mandate produces only tokens. The post-Agile era requires both.
